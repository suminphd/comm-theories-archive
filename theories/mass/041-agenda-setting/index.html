    Agenda-Setting Theory

    1) Executive Summary¶

    Agenda-Setting Theory, pioneered by Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw in 1972, explains how mass media influence public opinion by determining which issues the public thinks about rather than what the public thinks about those issues. The theory posits that media don't tell people what to think, but rather what to think about by controlling the salience of issues through coverage decisions. The core mechanism involves transfer of salience from the media agenda (issues emphasized in news coverage) to the public agenda (issues the public considers important). First-level agenda-setting focuses on object salience - which issues receive attention, while second-level agenda-setting examines attribute salience - which aspects or characteristics of issues are emphasized. The theory operates through several key processes: accessibility (making issues mentally available), applicability (making issues seem relevant for judgment), and priming (making issues more influential in evaluations). Contingent conditions affecting agenda-setting include issue characteristics (obtrusiveness, abstractness, duration), individual differences (political interest, media use patterns), media factors (coverage intensity, placement, framing), and social context (interpersonal discussion, elite cues). The theory has evolved through multiple generations, incorporating attribute agenda-setting, network agenda-setting, and intermedia agenda-setting concepts. Applications span political campaigns, public policy formation, crisis communication, social movements, and health communication, with particular relevance for understanding democratic processes and public sphere dynamics. Research demonstrates consistent agenda-setting effects across diverse contexts, though effect sizes vary based on issue type, media environment, and audience characteristics.

    2) Definition & Scope¶

    • Definition: The process by which mass media influence the relative importance that audiences assign to various public issues through selective attention and emphasis in news coverage, thereby shaping the public agenda of concerns and priorities.
    • Scope: Media influence contexts including news coverage effects on public opinion, political communication, policy agenda formation, crisis and issue management, social movement mobilization, and any situation where media attention affects public perceptions of issue importance.

    3) Key Constructs & Propositions¶

    Agenda-Setting Levels and Processes¶

    First-Level Agenda-Setting (Object Salience)¶

    Definition: The transfer of salience regarding which issues or objects receive public attention from media agenda to public agenda

    Object Salience Mechanisms: - Coverage Volume: Frequency and amount of coverage devoted to specific issues - Prominence Placement: Position of stories (front page, lead story, headlines) - Repetition Patterns: Consistent coverage over time creating cumulative effects - Cross-Media Consistency: Similar emphasis across different media outlets - Visual Emphasis: Use of images, graphics, and visual elements to highlight issues

    Issue Characteristics Affecting Salience Transfer: - Obtrusiveness: Issues directly experienced by audiences vs. those known only through media - Concreteness: Specific, tangible issues vs. abstract policy debates - Temporal Scope: Immediate events vs. long-term trends and developments - Geographic Proximity: Local vs. national vs. international issues - Human Interest: Issues involving personal stories vs. institutional processes

    Measurement Approaches: - Media Content Analysis: Quantitative analysis of coverage volume, placement, and emphasis - Public Opinion Surveys: Measuring public perceptions of issue importance and priority - Most Important Problem: Open-ended questions about top public concerns - Issue Priority Rankings: Forced-choice rankings of multiple issues - Longitudinal Tracking: Time-series analysis of media coverage and public opinion

    Second-Level Agenda-Setting (Attribute Salience)¶

    Definition: The transfer of salience regarding which attributes, aspects, or characteristics of issues are emphasized and how they are portrayed

    Attribute Dimensions: - Substantive Attributes: Factual information, policy details, and concrete characteristics - Affective Attributes: Emotional tone, evaluative judgments, and attitudinal orientations - Causal Attributes: Explanations for problems, responsibility assignments, and solution frameworks - Temporal Attributes: Time frames, urgency levels, and historical contexts - Spatial Attributes: Geographic scope, affected populations, and territorial dimensions

    Framing and Attribute Agenda-Setting: - Frame Salience: Which interpretive frameworks receive emphasis in coverage - Aspect Emphasis: Which dimensions of complex issues are highlighted or minimized - Valence Transfer: Positive, negative, or neutral evaluations of issue attributes - Competing Frames: How different interpretations compete for salience - Frame Ownership: Which sources successfully establish dominant attributions

    Cognitive and Affective Processing: - Accessibility Effects: Making certain attributes more mentally available for consideration - Applicability Effects: Making attributes seem more relevant for evaluating issues - Chronic Accessibility: Long-term availability of frequently emphasized attributes - Emotional Priming: How affective attributes influence emotional responses to issues - Evaluative Implications: How attribute emphasis affects overall issue evaluations

    Media Agenda Formation¶

    Gatekeeping and News Selection¶

    Definition: Editorial processes determining which issues receive coverage and how they are presented to audiences

    Gatekeeping Factors: - News Values: Criteria for determining newsworthiness (timeliness, proximity, conflict, impact) - Resource Constraints: Limitations of time, space, budget, and personnel affecting coverage decisions - Organizational Routines: Established practices and procedures for news production - Source Relationships: Dependencies on official sources, experts, and information providers - Audience Expectations: Perceived interests and preferences of target audiences

    Institutional Influences: - Ownership Effects: Corporate interests and ideological orientations of media owners - Advertiser Pressure: Commercial considerations affecting coverage decisions - Government Relations: Official pressure, access considerations, and regulatory concerns - Professional Norms: Journalistic standards, ethics, and peer expectations - Competitive Dynamics: Inter-media competition for audiences and exclusives

    Inter-media Agenda-Setting¶

    Definition: How different media outlets influence each other's coverage priorities and issue emphasis

    Influence Patterns: - Elite Media Influence: Prestige outlets setting coverage priorities for other media - Wire Service Effects: News agencies distributing standardized coverage priorities - Social Media Amplification: Online platforms affecting traditional media coverage decisions - Specialized Media Impact: Trade publications and niche outlets influencing mainstream coverage - Cross-Platform Convergence: Integration across multiple media platforms and formats

    Network Agenda-Setting: - Information Flow Networks: Patterns of information sharing among media organizations - Source Networks: Shared reliance on common information sources and experts - Professional Networks: Journalist interactions affecting coverage coordination - Technology Networks: Digital platforms facilitating rapid information dissemination - Audience Networks: Social media users creating feedback loops affecting media coverage

    Public Agenda Formation¶

    Individual-Level Processes¶

    Definition: Psychological mechanisms through which media coverage influences individual perceptions of issue importance

    Cognitive Mechanisms: - Accessibility Principle: Recently and frequently covered issues are more mentally available - Availability Heuristic: Using ease of recall as indicator of issue importance - Mere Exposure Effect: Repeated coverage increasing familiarity and perceived significance - Attention Economics: Limited cognitive resources creating competition among issues - Memory Decay: Declining salience of issues without continued media reinforcement

    Moderating Factors: - Personal Experience: Direct experience with issues reducing media influence - Prior Knowledge: Existing information affecting receptivity to media messages - Political Interest: Higher involvement increasing attention to political coverage - Media Dependency: Reliance on media for information about specific issues - Social Networks: Interpersonal discussion mediating media influence

    Social-Level Processes¶

    Definition: Collective mechanisms through which media influence aggregate public opinion and social priorities

    Social Construction Processes: - Collective Attention: Media creating shared focus on particular issues - Public Discourse: Media coverage shaping parameters of public debate - Social Reality Construction: Media defining which issues constitute social problems - Legitimation Effects: Media attention conferring importance and credibility - Agenda Spillover: Issue prominence affecting related concerns and priorities

    Democratic Implications: - Public Sphere Function: Media role in facilitating democratic deliberation - Issue Cycling: Patterns of attention rise and decline affecting policy windows - Representation Quality: Whether media agendas reflect diverse public interests - Elite-Public Connections: Media mediating relationships between leaders and citizens - Civic Engagement: How agenda-setting affects political participation and activism

    Contingent Conditions and Moderators¶

    Issue Characteristics¶

    Definition: Properties of issues that affect the strength and pattern of agenda-setting effects

    Obtrusiveness Levels: - Obtrusive Issues: Problems directly experienced by significant portions of the public (unemployment, crime, inflation) - Unobtrusive Issues: Problems known primarily through media coverage (foreign policy, environmental issues, complex policy debates) - Mixed Obtrusiveness: Issues with both direct experience and media-mediated components

    Issue Complexity: - Simple Issues: Easily understood problems with clear causes and solutions - Complex Issues: Multi-faceted problems requiring explanation and context - Technical Issues: Specialized knowledge requirements affecting public comprehension - Valence Issues: Broad consensus on goals with disagreement on means - Position Issues: Fundamental disagreement on appropriate policies

    Audience Factors¶

    Definition: Individual and group characteristics that moderate agenda-setting effects

    Demographic Moderators: - Education Levels: Higher education associated with greater agenda-setting effects for complex issues - Age Differences: Generational variations in media use and issue prioritization - Income Effects: Economic status affecting attention to different issue types - Geographic Location: Urban-rural differences in issue relevance and media exposure - Political Affiliation: Partisan differences in receptivity to agenda-setting influences

    Psychological Moderators: - Need for Orientation: Individual differences in information seeking and processing - Political Interest: Higher involvement increasing susceptibility to agenda-setting effects - Media Habits: Usage patterns affecting exposure to agenda-setting influences - Cognitive Style: Processing preferences affecting response to media emphasis - Social Identity: Group memberships affecting issue relevance and attention

    Core Propositions¶

    P1: Media influence public opinion by transferring issue salience from media agenda to public agenda

    P2: First-level agenda-setting determines which issues receive public attention

    P3: Second-level agenda-setting influences which attributes of issues are considered salient

    P4: Agenda-setting effects are stronger for unobtrusive than obtrusive issues

    P5: Media coverage frequency, prominence, and consistency enhance salience transfer

    P6: Individual differences in need for orientation moderate agenda-setting effects

    P7: Agenda-setting operates through accessibility and applicability mechanisms

    P8: Inter-media agenda-setting creates coordination in coverage priorities across outlets

    5) Boundary Conditions & Assumptions¶

    Core Assumptions¶

    • Limited Attention: Public has limited capacity to attend to multiple issues simultaneously
    • Media Dependence: Audiences rely on media for information about many issues, especially unobtrusive ones
    • Cognitive Accessibility: Frequently covered issues become more mentally available for public consideration
    • Rational Information Processing: People use media emphasis as cue for issue importance
    • Democratic Context: Theory assumes relatively free media system and public opinion influence on policy

    Boundary Conditions¶

    • Media System Characteristics: Theory most applicable in systems with editorial independence and competitive media
    • Issue Type Constraints: Effects stronger for unobtrusive issues with limited direct public experience
    • Time Frame Limitations: Agenda-setting effects require sustained coverage over time to develop
    • Audience Engagement: Effects depend on sufficient media attention and public interest in news
    • Information Environment: Theory developed for traditional media; digital media create different dynamics
    • Cultural Context: Effects may vary across cultures with different media usage patterns and authority relationships
    • Political System Features: Democratic institutions and norms affect how agenda-setting translates to policy
    • Crisis Conditions: Emergency situations may disrupt normal agenda-setting processes

    6) Operationalization & Measurement¶

    Construct Typical Operationalization Example Items Measurement Notes
    Media Agenda Content analysis of coverage "Number of stories about issue X per week" Weight by prominence, length, placement
    Public Agenda Survey measures of issue importance "What is the most important problem facing the nation?" Use both open-ended and closed-ended questions
    Issue Salience Ranking or rating scales "How important is this issue to you?" (1-10) Include multiple issues for comparative analysis
    Attribute Emphasis Content analysis of frames/attributes "Percentage of stories emphasizing attribute Y" Code for substantive and affective attributes
    Need for Orientation Composite scale "I need to know what's going on in politics" (1-7) Combine relevance and uncertainty measures
    Media Exposure Self-report or usage tracking "How often do you watch television news?" Include multiple media types and platforms

    Longitudinal Agenda-Setting Design¶

    Time-Series Analysis Approach: - Media Tracking: Continuous content analysis over extended periods (6-12 months) - Public Opinion Waves: Regular surveys measuring issue importance and concern - Cross-Lagged Correlation: Testing temporal precedence of media influence on public opinion - Trend Analysis: Identifying patterns of issue rise and decline in both agendas - Intervention Analysis: Examining how external events affect agenda-setting processes

    Field Experiments and Natural Experiments¶

    Media Market Comparisons: - Geographic Variation: Comparing communities with different media coverage patterns - Media Strike Studies: Examining agenda-setting during newspaper strikes or media blackouts - Election Campaign Analysis: Natural variation in coverage across different media markets - Policy Implementation Studies: Comparing agenda-setting effects across different jurisdictions

    Computational Approaches¶

    Big Data Agenda-Setting Analysis: - Automated Content Analysis: Machine learning approaches to classify and measure media coverage - Social Media Mining: Analysis of Twitter trends, Facebook discussions, and online attention patterns - Google Trends Analysis: Search behavior as indicator of public agenda priorities - Network Analysis: Mapping information flow patterns across media and social networks

    8) Findings & Effect Patterns¶

    Meta-Analytic Evidence¶

    Overall Effect Sizes: - Aggregate Studies: Average correlation between media and public agendas r = .53 (Wanta & Ghanem, 2007) - Individual-Level Studies: Average effect size d = .35 for agenda-setting on issue importance ratings - Time-Lag Effects: Optimal media influence typically occurs 4-6 weeks after coverage begins - Issue Variation: Effects range from r = .20 to r = .80 depending on issue characteristics

    Methodological Moderators: - Study Design: Experimental studies show larger effects (d = .42) than survey research (d = .28) - Measurement Approach: Open-ended questions show stronger effects than closed-ended ratings - Time Frame: Studies with longer observation periods show more stable effect patterns - Sample Characteristics: Effects stronger in samples with higher political interest

    Temporal Dynamics¶

    Issue Attention Cycles: - Issue Discovery: Initial media attention following dramatic events or elite initiatives - Alarmed Discovery: Intensive coverage creating public awareness and concern - Cost Recognition: Coverage of implementation difficulties and trade-offs - Declining Interest: Public attention fatigue and media movement to new issues - Post-Problem Stage: Sporadic coverage and institutionalized policy responses

    Coverage Patterns: - Threshold Effects: Minimum coverage levels needed to affect public agenda - Saturation Effects: Diminishing returns from excessive coverage - Intermittency Effects: How gaps in coverage affect agenda maintenance - Reinforcement Needs: Ongoing coverage required to sustain agenda effects

    Individual Difference Effects¶

    Need for Orientation: - High Relevance/High Uncertainty: Strongest agenda-setting effects (r = .70-.80) - High Relevance/Low Uncertainty: Moderate effects due to existing knowledge (r = .40-.50) - Low Relevance/High Uncertainty: Weak effects due to limited interest (r = .20-.30) - Low Relevance/Low Uncertainty: Minimal agenda-setting effects (r = .10-.20)

    Political Engagement: - High Political Interest: Stronger agenda-setting effects for political issues - Media Attention Patterns: Engaged citizens more responsive to coverage changes - Issue Complexity: Educated audiences show stronger effects for complex issues - Partisan Filtering: Party identification moderates agenda-setting for partisan issues

    Media System Effects¶

    Outlet Characteristics: - Prestige Media: Stronger agenda-setting effects than local or tabloid media - Television vs. Print: Television shows stronger effects for visual and dramatic issues - Online Media: Different agenda-setting patterns with algorithmic content curation - Social Media: Amplification and modification of traditional agenda-setting processes

    Competition and Diversity: - Media Concentration: Reduced diversity weakens agenda-setting for minority issues - Inter-Media Competition: Competition can either amplify or fragment agenda-setting effects - Source Diversity: Multiple perspectives reduce uniform agenda-setting effects - Alternative Media: Challenge to mainstream media agenda-setting power

    9) Applications¶

    Political Campaign Communication¶

    Election Campaign Agenda-Setting: - Issue Prioritization: Campaigns attempt to focus media and public attention on favorable issues - Strategic Communication: Timing announcements to capture media attention and set agenda - Opposition Research: Highlighting opponent weaknesses to shift media focus - Media Relations: Building relationships with journalists to influence coverage priorities

    10) Comparative Lens¶

    Theory Core Focus Relationship to Agenda-Setting Key Differences
    Framing Theory How issues are presented and interpreted Second-level agenda-setting overlaps with framing Framing focuses on interpretation; agenda-setting on attention
    Priming Theory How media affect evaluation criteria Priming follows from agenda-setting effects Priming concerns judgment standards; agenda-setting concerns issue importance
    Gatekeeping Theory Media selection of news content Gatekeeping determines media agenda formation Gatekeeping focuses on selection; agenda-setting on effects
    Spiral of Silence Public opinion formation and expression Both examine media influence on public opinion Spiral focuses on opinion climate; agenda-setting on issue salience

    Agenda-Setting vs. Framing Theory¶

    Conceptual Relationship: - First-Level Agenda-Setting: Determines which objects (issues) receive attention - Second-Level Agenda-Setting: Influences which attributes of objects are salient - Framing: Shapes how issues are understood and interpreted by audiences - Overlap Zone: Second-level agenda-setting and framing both address attribute salience

    Key Distinctions: - Cognitive Focus: Agenda-setting emphasizes accessibility; framing emphasizes applicability - Process Emphasis: Agenda-setting about attention allocation; framing about meaning construction
    - Measurement Approach: Agenda-setting uses salience rankings; framing uses interpretive analysis - Temporal Dynamics: Agenda-setting tracks issue rise/decline; framing examines interpretive stability

    Integration Possibilities: - Sequential Process: Agenda-setting creates attention, framing shapes interpretation - Complementary Analysis: Using both theories to understand complete media influence process - Attribute Framing: Combining second-level agenda-setting with frame analysis - Multi-Level Models: Integrating object salience, attribute salience, and frame effects

    Agenda-Setting vs. Priming Theory¶

    Theoretical Connection: - Accessibility-Applicability Model: Agenda-setting creates accessibility; priming involves applicability - Causal Sequence: Media attention (agenda-setting) → cognitive availability (priming) → evaluation (effects) - Memory Processes: Both theories rely on activation and retrieval of information from memory - Political Context: Both theories particularly relevant for understanding political communication effects

    Conceptual Differences: - Primary Focus: Agenda-setting on issue importance; priming on evaluation criteria - Dependent Variables: Agenda-setting measures salience; priming measures evaluation changes - Time Frame: Agenda-setting longer-term attention patterns; priming immediate evaluation effects - Scope: Agenda-setting broad public opinion; priming specific judgment processes

    Research Integration: - Sequential Studies: Testing agenda-setting as precursor to priming effects - Mediational Models: Examining how agenda-setting enables priming processes - Issue-Specific Analysis: How different issues show varying agenda-setting and priming patterns - Individual Differences: Moderators affecting both agenda-setting and priming susceptibility

    Agenda-Setting vs. Gatekeeping Theory¶

    Process Relationship: - Sequential Stages: Gatekeeping → Media Agenda → Agenda-Setting Effects → Public Agenda - Editorial Decisions: Gatekeeping determines what becomes available for agenda-setting - Selection Mechanisms: Gatekeeping filters; agenda-setting amplifies selected content - Institutional Analysis: Both theories examine media organizational processes and effects

    Analytical Focus: - Gatekeeping Emphasis: Internal media processes and decision-making criteria - Agenda-Setting Emphasis: External effects on audiences and public opinion - Research Methods: Gatekeeping uses newsroom ethnography; agenda-setting uses content analysis and surveys - Policy Implications: Gatekeeping informs media regulation; agenda-setting informs democratic theory

    Agenda-Setting and Digital Media Evolution¶

    Traditional Media Assumptions: - Limited Channels: Scarcity of media outlets creating concentrated agenda-setting power - Professional Gatekeepers: Editors and journalists controlling information flow - Mass Audiences: Large, relatively homogeneous audiences receiving similar content - Linear Process: Clear sequence from media agenda to public agenda

    Digital Age Modifications: - Algorithmic Gatekeeping: Automated systems affecting what content receives attention - Fragmented Audiences: Personalized news feeds creating individual agenda-setting experiences - Social Media Amplification: User sharing and discussion affecting agenda formation - Real-Time Feedback: Immediate audience response influencing media coverage decisions

    Theoretical Adaptations: - Network Agenda-Setting: Multiple interconnected agendas across platforms and communities - Intermedia Agenda-Setting: How different media platforms influence each other - Participatory Agenda-Setting: Audience role in creating and promoting issues - Algorithmic Agenda-Setting: How recommendation systems affect attention patterns

    11) Critiques & Debates¶

    Major Criticisms¶

    Causal Direction Questions: - Critics argue that media may follow rather than lead public opinion on many issues - Public interest and concern may drive media coverage rather than media creating public interest - Difficulty establishing clear temporal precedence in observational studies - Feedback loops between media and public agendas complicate causal inference

    Democratic Assumptions: - Theory assumes media agenda-setting serves democratic functions but may not always do so - Elite control of media agendas may undermine rather than enhance democratic deliberation - Market-driven news selection may prioritize profit over public interest - Concentration of media ownership limiting diversity of agenda-setting influences

    Methodological Limitations: - Heavy reliance on correlation studies without clear causal evidence - Measurement challenges in defining and operationalizing "agenda" concepts - Artificial separation of media and public agendas that may be more interconnected - Limited attention to individual variation in agenda-setting susceptibility

    Cultural and Social Context: - Theory developed in Western democratic contexts may not apply universally - Limited consideration of how social class, race, and gender affect agenda-setting processes - Insufficient attention to alternative information sources and community-based agendas - Assumption of passive audiences not reflecting active information seeking and processing

    Contemporary Debates¶

    Digital Media Transformation: - Algorithmic Filtering: How personalized content delivery affects traditional agenda-setting processes - Information Fragmentation: Whether digital media create unified or fragmented public agendas - Social Media Influence: Role of platforms like Twitter and Facebook in modern agenda-setting - Misinformation Spread: How false information affects agenda-setting dynamics and public priorities

    Globalization and Transnational Agendas: - Cross-Border Information Flow: How international media influence domestic agenda-setting - Global vs. Local Issues: Competition between international and community concerns for agenda space - Cultural Imperialism: Whether dominant media systems impose agenda priorities on other cultures - Social Movement Coordination: How global movements use media to coordinate agenda-setting across countries

    Political Polarization Effects: - Partisan Media: How ideologically oriented media create different agendas for different audiences - Echo Chambers: Whether audience segmentation undermines unified agenda-setting effects - Selective Exposure: How audience preferences for confirming information affect agenda reception - Elite Polarization: Whether political elite disagreement reduces media agenda coherence

    Crisis and Disruption Events: - Emergency Override: How crises disrupt normal agenda-setting processes and create unified attention - Attention Competition: Whether multiple simultaneous crises fragment or focus agenda-setting - Recovery Patterns: How public and media attention return to normal patterns after disruptions - Institutional Trust: Whether crisis events enhance or undermine media agenda-setting credibility

    Theoretical Development Directions¶

    Multi-Level Integration: - Individual-Institutional Models: Connecting personal agenda-setting with organizational and societal processes - Cross-Cultural Validation: Testing agenda-setting theory across diverse political and media systems - Temporal Dynamics: Better understanding of how agenda-setting effects develop and decay over time - Network Approaches: Modeling agenda-setting as network phenomena with multiple interconnected actors

    Technology and Innovation: - Artificial Intelligence: How AI systems affect agenda formation and information distribution - Virtual Reality: Whether immersive media create different agenda-setting dynamics - Blockchain Media: How decentralized information systems might affect traditional agenda-setting - Augmented Reality: Integration of digital information with physical environments affecting attention patterns

    Measurement and Methodology: - Real-Time Tracking: Using digital data to measure agenda changes in real-time rather than retrospectively - Physiological Indicators: Incorporating attention, arousal, and engagement measures beyond self-report - Computational Methods: Using machine learning and AI to analyze large-scale agenda patterns - Experimental Innovation: Developing more realistic experimental paradigms for testing agenda-setting effects

def agenda_setting_analysis():
    """Analyze agenda-setting relationships between media and public agendas"""

    # Generate sample agenda-setting data
    np.random.seed(42)
    n_issues = 10
    n_weeks = 20

    issues = ['Economy', 'Healthcare', 'Education', 'Crime', 'Environment', 
              'Immigration', 'Defense', 'Infrastructure', 'Energy', 'Technology']

    # Simulate media agenda data (coverage volume)
    media_agenda_data = []
    for week in range(n_weeks):
        # Create realistic media coverage patterns with some issues getting more attention
        base_coverage = np.random.exponential(scale=2, size=n_issues)
        # Add some issues that become "hot" topics
        if week > 8 and week < 15:  # Economic crisis simulation
            base_coverage[0] *= 3  # Economy gets much more coverage
        if week > 12:  # Healthcare debate simulation  
            base_coverage[1] *= 2  # Healthcare gets more coverage

        week_data = {
            'week': week + 1,
            'issue': issues,
            'media_coverage': base_coverage
        }
        media_agenda_data.extend([
            {'week': week + 1, 'issue': issue, 'media_coverage': coverage}
            for issue, coverage in zip(issues, base_coverage)
        ])

    media_df = pd.DataFrame(media_agenda_data)

    # Simulate public agenda data (with 2-week lag from media influence)
    public_agenda_data = []
    for week in range(n_weeks):
        # Public agenda influenced by media agenda from 2 weeks prior
        if week >= 2:
            prior_media = media_df[media_df['week'] == week - 1]['media_coverage'].values
            # Add noise and individual variation
            public_concern = prior_media * 0.7 + np.random.normal(0, 0.5, n_issues)
            public_concern = np.maximum(public_concern, 0)  # Ensure positive values
        else:
            # Initial weeks with baseline public concern
            public_concern = np.random.exponential(scale=1.5, size=n_issues)

        public_agenda_data.extend([
            {'week': week + 1, 'issue': issue, 'concern': concern}
            for issue, concern in zip(issues, public_concern)
        ])

    public_df = pd.DataFrame(public_agenda_data)

    return media_df, public_df

def calculate_agenda_setting_effects(media_df, public_df):
    """Calculate various agenda-setting effect measures"""

    results = {}

    # Weekly correlations between media and public agendas
    weekly_correlations = []
    for week in range(1, media_df['week'].max() + 1):
        media_week = media_df[media_df['week'] == week]['media_coverage'].values
        public_week = public_df[public_df['week'] == week]['concern'].values

        if len(media_week) == len(public_week):
            corr, p_value = spearmanr(media_week, public_week)
            weekly_correlations.append({
                'week': week,
                'correlation': corr,
                'p_value': p_value
            })

    correlation_df = pd.DataFrame(weekly_correlations)
    results['weekly_correlations'] = correlation_df

    # Overall agenda-setting correlation
    media_ranks = media_df.groupby('issue')['media_coverage'].mean().rank(ascending=False)
    public_ranks = public_df.groupby('issue')['concern'].mean().rank(ascending=False)

    overall_corr, p_val = spearmanr(media_ranks, public_ranks)
    results['overall_correlation'] = {
        'correlation': overall_corr,
        'p_value': p_val,
        'media_agenda': media_ranks.to_dict(),
        'public_agenda': public_ranks.to_dict()
    }

    # Cross-lagged correlations (testing different time lags)
    lag_correlations = []
    for lag in range(1, 5):
        lag_corrs = []
        for week in range(lag + 1, media_df['week'].max() + 1):
            media_week = media_df[media_df['week'] == week - lag]['media_coverage'].values
            public_week = public_df[public_df['week'] == week]['concern'].values

            if len(media_week) == len(public_week):
                corr, _ = spearmanr(media_week, public_week)
                lag_corrs.append(corr)

        if lag_corrs:
            lag_correlations.append({
                'lag_weeks': lag,
                'mean_correlation': np.mean(lag_corrs),
                'correlation_count': len(lag_corrs)
            })

    results['lag_analysis'] = pd.DataFrame(lag_correlations)

    return results

def visualize_agenda_setting_effects(media_df, public_df, results):
    """Create visualizations of agenda-setting patterns"""

    fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(15, 10))
