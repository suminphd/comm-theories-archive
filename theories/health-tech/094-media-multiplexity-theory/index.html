    Media Multiplexity Theory

    1) Executive Summary¶

    Media Multiplexity Theory (MMT) explains the relationship between communication channel diversity and social tie strength, proposing that individuals with stronger social relationships communicate through more different types of media channels. Developed by Caroline Haythornthwaite (2005), the theory addresses how people select and combine various communication technologies in their interpersonal relationships. The core proposition states that tie strength (the closeness and intensity of social relationships) is positively correlated with media multiplexity (the number of different communication channels used between two people). Strong ties (close friends, family members, intimate partners) typically communicate through multiple channels including face-to-face interaction, phone calls, text messages, email, social media, and other platforms. Weak ties (acquaintances, distant colleagues, casual contacts) tend to rely on fewer, often more formal communication channels. The theory suggests that channel diversity serves multiple functions: providing redundancy for important communications, accommodating different communication needs and contexts, enabling more frequent contact opportunities, and supporting relationship maintenance across various situations. Digital proliferation has expanded the range of available channels, making multiplexity more accessible and varied. The theory has significant implications for understanding relationship development, organizational communication, social network analysis, and technology adoption patterns. Research shows that multiplexity patterns can predict relationship outcomes, influence information flow in networks, and affect collaborative effectiveness in both personal and professional contexts.

    2) Definition & Scope¶

    • Definition: A communication theory proposing that the number of different media channels used for communication between two individuals is positively related to the strength of their social relationship, with stronger ties characterized by greater media diversity and multiplexity.
    • Scope: Interpersonal communication across all relationship types (personal, professional, family, friendship), encompassing both traditional and digital communication channels, and applicable to various contexts including organizational, educational, and social network environments.

    3) Key Constructs & Propositions¶

    Media Multiplexity¶

    Definition and Conceptualization¶

    Media Multiplexity: The number of different communication channels or media platforms used for interaction between two individuals in a social relationship

    Channel Types: - Synchronous Channels: Real-time communication requiring simultaneous participation (face-to-face, phone calls, video calls, instant messaging) - Asynchronous Channels: Time-delayed communication allowing flexible response timing (email, text messages, social media posts, voice messages) - Formal Channels: Structured, official communication platforms (work email, official messaging systems, formal meetings) - Informal Channels: Casual, personal communication platforms (personal social media, casual conversations, informal text messaging)

    Multiplexity Measurement: - Channel Count: Simple enumeration of different media types used between relationship partners - Weighted Multiplexity: Accounting for frequency of use across different channels - Channel Diversity Index: Mathematical measures considering both variety and distribution of communication across channels - Contextual Multiplexity: Channel use variation across different situational contexts

    Channel Selection Factors¶

    Accessibility and Availability: - Technology Access: Available devices and platforms for both communication partners - Digital Literacy: Skill and comfort levels with different communication technologies - Time Constraints: Available time for synchronous vs. asynchronous communication - Geographic Factors: Physical distance affecting channel preference and necessity

    Message Characteristics: - Content Sensitivity: Private, personal, or confidential information requiring secure channels - Urgency Level: Time-critical messages influencing channel selection - Message Complexity: Detailed or nuanced communication needs affecting medium choice - Emotional Content: Expressive or emotional messages requiring rich communication channels

    Relationship Context: - Social Appropriateness: Culturally and socially acceptable channels for specific relationship types - Power Dynamics: Hierarchical relationships affecting communication channel choices - Relationship History: Established communication patterns and preferences between individuals - Mutual Preferences: Shared comfort and preference for specific communication platforms

    Tie Strength¶

    Definition and Dimensions¶

    Tie Strength: The intensity, closeness, and significance of the social relationship between two individuals, encompassing emotional, instrumental, and social dimensions

    Granovetter's Tie Strength Components: - Emotional Intensity: Depth of feeling and emotional connection between relationship partners - Intimacy: Level of personal disclosure, trust, and vulnerability in the relationship - Time Investment: Amount of time regularly spent in interaction and relationship maintenance - Reciprocal Services: Exchange of support, favors, and assistance between relationship partners

    Relationship Categories: - Strong Ties: Close family members, intimate friends, romantic partners, best friends - Moderate Ties: Good friends, close colleagues, regular social contacts, supportive acquaintances - Weak Ties: Casual acquaintances, distant colleagues, infrequent social contacts, professional contacts

    Tie Strength Indicators¶

    Behavioral Measures: - Communication Frequency: How often individuals interact across all channels - Response Time: Speed of response to communications indicating relationship priority - Initiation Patterns: Who initiates contact and how frequently - Duration of Interactions: Length of time spent in communication episodes

    Content Measures: - Topic Breadth: Range of subjects discussed between relationship partners - Personal Disclosure: Level of intimate or personal information shared - Support Exchange: Frequency and type of emotional or instrumental support provided - Shared Activities: Joint participation in social, recreational, or professional activities

    Affective Measures: - Emotional Closeness: Self-reported feelings of connection and affection - Trust Levels: Confidence in the other person's reliability and good intentions - Relationship Satisfaction: Overall positive evaluation of the relationship - Commitment: Investment in maintaining and developing the relationship

    Multiplexity-Tie Strength Relationship¶

    Core Theoretical Proposition¶

    Primary Hypothesis: Stronger social ties are associated with greater media multiplexity, with individuals using more diverse communication channels to maintain closer relationships

    Causal Mechanisms: - Relationship Investment: Stronger relationships justify investment in learning and using multiple communication channels - Communication Needs: Close relationships require diverse communication for different purposes and contexts - Redundancy Function: Important relationships benefit from backup communication channels - Convenience Optimization: Multiple channels provide flexibility for maintaining frequent contact

    Multiplexity Functions in Strong Ties¶

    Relationship Maintenance: - Frequent Contact: Multiple channels enable more opportunities for interaction - Contextual Adaptation: Different channels suit different situations and communication needs - Relationship Depth: Various channels support different types of intimate and personal communication - Continuity Assurance: Alternative channels provide backup when primary channels are unavailable

    Communication Efficiency: - Message Appropriateness: Matching message content to optimal communication channel - Urgency Management: Using appropriate channels for time-sensitive vs. non-urgent communication - Attention Management: Distributing communication across channels to avoid overwhelming any single platform - Social Coordination: Using multiple channels to coordinate complex social interactions

    Social Support Functions: - Emotional Support: Different channels facilitate various types of emotional expression and support - Instrumental Support: Practical assistance coordination through appropriate communication channels - Information Sharing: Diverse channels enable comprehensive information exchange - Social Integration: Multiple channels maintain connection across different social contexts

    Organizational vs. Personal Contexts¶

    Workplace Multiplexity¶

    Professional Communication Patterns: - Formal-Informal Balance: Strong workplace ties often combine formal work channels with informal personal channels - Hierarchical Considerations: Power dynamics affecting appropriate channel selection and multiplexity - Task Coordination: Multiple channels supporting complex project collaboration and coordination - Social Relationship Development: Personal channels supplementing professional communication for relationship building

    Organizational Factors: - Technology Provision: Company-provided communication tools and platforms - Communication Policies: Organizational rules governing appropriate channel use - Work Culture: Informal norms about mixing personal and professional communication - Geographic Distribution: Remote work and distributed teams affecting channel necessity

    Personal Relationship Multiplexity¶

    Family Communication: - Generational Differences: Varying comfort and preference for different communication technologies across age groups - Family Dynamics: Role relationships affecting communication channel appropriateness - Geographic Distance: Physical separation increasing need for multiple communication channels - Life Stage Factors: Different life phases affecting communication needs and channel preferences

    Friendship Networks: - Social Group Coordination: Multiple channels facilitating group communication and event coordination - Peer Influence: Social pressure to adopt new communication platforms popular within friend groups - Activity-Based Communication: Different channels supporting various shared activities and interests - Relationship Evolution: Channel diversity changing as friendships develop or decline

    Digital Era Considerations¶

    Platform Proliferation¶

    Communication Technology Expansion: - Social Media Platforms: Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, TikTok, LinkedIn enabling diverse communication types - Messaging Applications: WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal, Discord providing specialized messaging features - Video Communication: Zoom, Skype, FaceTime facilitating face-to-face interaction at distance - Collaborative Platforms: Slack, Microsoft Teams, Google Workspace supporting work-related multiplexity

    Platform Specialization: - Content-Specific Platforms: Different platforms optimized for different communication content types - Audience Segmentation: Using different platforms for different social circles and relationship types - Feature Integration: Platforms combining multiple communication modes (text, voice, video, file sharing) - Cross-Platform Communication: Integration allowing communication across different platform ecosystems

    Digital Native Considerations¶

    Generational Differences: - Technology Comfort: Younger generations more comfortable with high multiplexity communication patterns - Platform Switching: Ability to rapidly adopt new platforms and integrate them into existing relationship patterns - Digital Relationship Maintenance: Using technology as primary means of maintaining social relationships - Multi-Modal Communication: Simultaneously using multiple channels for single conversations or interactions

    Core Propositions¶

    P1: The number of communication channels used between two individuals is positively correlated with the strength of their social relationship

    P2: Strong ties use more diverse communication channels than weak ties

    P3: Media multiplexity serves relationship maintenance functions including redundancy, convenience, and contextual appropriateness

    P4: Organizational relationships show different multiplexity patterns than personal relationships due to formal constraints and professional norms

    P5: The availability of new communication technologies increases overall multiplexity potential for all relationship types

    P6: Multiplexity patterns can predict relationship outcomes and stability over time

    P7: Cultural and demographic factors moderate the relationship between tie strength and media multiplexity

    5) Boundary Conditions & Assumptions¶

    Core Assumptions¶

    • Channel Availability: Assumes access to multiple communication technologies for relationship partners
    • Voluntary Communication: Theory applies to chosen communication rather than mandatory interaction
    • Relationship Choice: Assumes individuals have agency in selecting relationship investment levels
    • Technology Literacy: Basic competence in using available communication channels
    • Cultural Consistency: Shared understanding of appropriate communication channel use within social context

    Boundary Conditions¶

    • Technology Constraints: Limited applicability when communication options are severely restricted
    • Cultural Specificity: Effects may vary significantly across cultures with different communication norms
    • Relationship Involuntariness: Theory less applicable to mandatory relationships (some family, work situations)
    • Crisis Situations: Emergency or crisis communication may override normal multiplexity patterns
    • Age and Generational Factors: Older adults may show different multiplexity patterns due to technology comfort
    • Economic Constraints: Financial limitations on communication technology access may limit multiplexity options

    7) Research Designs & Analysis¶

    Cross-Sectional Network Study¶

    Social Network Analysis Design: - Participants: Complete social networks (organizations, communities, friend groups) - Measures: Full relationship matrix with tie strength and channel use for each relationship pair - Analysis: Network analysis software to examine multiplexity patterns across network structure - Visualization: Network graphs showing relationship strength and channel diversity

    Procedure: 1. Network Boundary Definition: Identify complete network for analysis (workplace, school, community) 2. Relationship Mapping: Survey all network members about their relationships with other members 3. Channel Assessment: Document communication channels used for each relationship pair 4. Tie Strength Measurement: Assess relationship closeness and interaction frequency 5. Network Analysis: Use network analysis software to examine patterns and correlations

    Longitudinal Relationship Development Study¶

    Panel Study Design: - Time Points: Multiple measurements over relationship development period (6-24 months) - Participants: Individuals in developing relationships (new employees, students, community members) - Tracking: Changes in both tie strength and media multiplexity over time - Analysis: Growth curve modeling and cross-lagged panel analysis

    Data Collection Waves: 

    Wave 1 (Baseline): Initial relationship assessment
Wave 2 (3 months): Early relationship development
Wave 3 (6 months): Relationship establishment
Wave 4 (12 months): Relationship stabilization
Wave 5 (18 months): Long-term relationship patterns

    Experimental Channel Introduction Study¶

    Technology Adoption Experiment: - Design: Introduce new communication channel to existing relationship networks - Manipulation: Provide access to new communication platform for subset of relationships - Measurement: Track adoption patterns and relationship effects - Comparison: Compare multiplexity changes for strong vs. weak ties

    Experimental Conditions: - High Tie Strength: Close friends/family given access to new communication platform - Low Tie Strength: Acquaintances/weak ties given access to same platform - Control Groups: No access to new platform for comparison - Mixed Conditions: Some relationships with asymmetric access to new platform

    8) Findings & Effect Patterns¶

    Basic Multiplexity-Tie Strength Relationships¶

    Correlation Patterns: - Strong Positive Correlation: Tie strength and media multiplexity typically correlate r = .40-.65 - Dose-Response Relationship: Each additional communication channel associated with increased relationship closeness - Threshold Effects: Minimal multiplexity differences among very weak ties, more variation among stronger ties - Ceiling Effects: Very strong ties may plateau in multiplexity due to channel availability limits

    Channel Usage by Tie Strength: - Strong Ties: Average 4-7 different communication channels (face-to-face, phone, text, email, social media, apps) - Moderate Ties: Average 2-4 communication channels (often excluding most personal channels) - Weak Ties: Average 1-2 communication channels (typically formal or single preferred channel) - Professional Ties: Show different patterns with more emphasis on formal channels regardless of closeness

    Contextual Variations¶

    Organizational Settings: - Workplace Relationships: Strong work ties average 3-5 channels (mixing professional and personal) - Hierarchical Effects: Superior-subordinate relationships show limited multiplexity regardless of closeness - Team Collaboration: Project teams develop higher multiplexity for coordination purposes - Remote Work: Distance amplifies multiplexity effects for maintaining strong workplace relationships

    Personal Relationships: - Family Relationships: Highest multiplexity levels (5-8 channels common for close family) - Romantic Relationships: Maximum multiplexity with use of all available channels plus private channels - Friendships: Moderate to high multiplexity depending on relationship closeness and shared activities - Geographic Distance: Long-distance relationships show higher multiplexity to compensate for physical separation

    Age and Generational Differences: - Digital Natives (Gen Z/Millennials): Higher baseline multiplexity across all relationship types - Generation X: Moderate multiplexity with preference for established channels - Baby Boomers: Lower multiplexity with focus on traditional channels (phone, email, face-to-face) - Cross-Generational Relationships: Multiplexity limited by least technologically comfortable person

    Channel-Specific Patterns¶

    Channel Popularity by Tie Strength: - Face-to-Face: Universal for strong ties, optional for weak ties - Phone Calls: High use in strong ties, formal use in moderate ties, rare in weak ties - Text Messaging: Very high in strong personal ties, moderate in professional ties - Email: Common across all tie strengths but primary channel for weak professional ties - Social Media: Varies by platform and age group, generally higher in stronger personal ties - Video Calls: Increasingly important for strong ties, especially during remote periods

    Platform Specialization: - Work Platforms (Slack, Teams): High multiplexity within organizational boundaries - Personal Platforms (Instagram, Snapchat): Reserved for stronger personal relationships - Cross-Platform Communication: Strongest ties often use platforms across personal-professional boundaries - Ephemeral Platforms: Intimate channels (disappearing messages) restricted to strongest ties

    Predictive Relationships¶

    Relationship Development: - Multiplexity Growth: Adding communication channels often precedes reported relationship strengthening - Channel Adoption Speed: Strong ties adopt new communication channels faster than weak ties - Relationship Decline: Reduction in multiplexity often signals relationship weakening - Maintenance Patterns: Stable multiplexity associated with stable relationship satisfaction

    Communication Effectiveness: - Information Transfer: Higher multiplexity associated with better information sharing and coordination - Conflict Resolution: Multiple channels provide more options for addressing relationship problems - Support Provision: Diverse channels enable more comprehensive social support delivery - Relationship Satisfaction: Multiplexity partially mediates relationship between tie strength and satisfaction

    Cultural and Demographic Moderators¶

    Cultural Context: - Individualistic Cultures: Higher multiplexity for personal relationships, clear personal-professional boundaries - Collectivistic Cultures: More overlap between professional and personal channels - High-Context Cultures: Greater emphasis on rich channels (face-to-face, video) even for weak ties - Technology-Advanced Cultures: Higher baseline multiplexity across all relationship types

    Socioeconomic Factors: - Higher SES: Greater access to diverse communication technologies enabling higher multiplexity - Lower SES: More constraint on multiplexity due to technology access limitations - Education Level: Higher education associated with more strategic channel selection - Urban vs. Rural: Urban settings typically show higher multiplexity due to technology infrastructure

    Theory Core Focus Relationship to Media Multiplexity Theory Key Differences
    Media Richness Theory Channel selection based on task requirements Both address media selection but MMT focuses on relationship context MRT task-focused; MMT relationship-focused
    Social Information Processing Theory Online relationship development over time Both examine how technology affects relationships SIPT focuses on relationship formation; MMT on channel diversity
    Uses and Gratifications Theory Individual media selection for need satisfaction Both explain media choice but MMT emphasizes relational factors U&G individual-focused; MMT relationship-focused
    Social Network Theory Structure and effects of social relationships MMT applies network concepts to communication patterns SNT broader; MMT specifically about communication channels

    11) Critiques & Debates¶

    Major Criticisms¶

    Correlation vs. Causation: Critics argue that the theory doesn't adequately address whether multiplexity causes stronger relationships or strong relationships lead to multiplexity, creating potential for circular reasoning

    Cultural Specificity: The theory was developed primarily in Western, technology-rich contexts and may not generalize to cultures with different communication norms or technology access patterns

    Channel Definition Problems: Distinguishing between different "channels" becomes problematic as platforms integrate multiple communication modes and new hybrid platforms emerge

    Temporal Assumptions: The theory may not account for how rapidly changing technology landscapes affect the stability of multiplexity patterns over time

    Contemporary Debates¶

    Digital Platform Evolution: - Platform Convergence: How do integrated platforms (combining text, voice, video, file sharing) affect multiplexity measurement? - Cross-Platform Communication: Do conversations spanning multiple platforms count as single or multiple channels? - AI-Mediated Communication: How do smart assistants and automated systems affect human-to-human multiplexity patterns? - Virtual Reality Communication: Will immersive technologies create new categories of communication channels?

def analyze_media_multiplexity(relationship_data):
    """
    Comprehensive analysis of media multiplexity patterns in social networks

    Parameters:
    relationship_data (DataFrame): Data with relationship info and channel usage

    Returns:
    dict: Complete analysis results including multiplexity patterns and predictions
    """

    # Calculate multiplexity scores
    channel_columns = [col for col in relationship_data.columns if 'channel_' in col]
    if channel_columns:
        relationship_data['multiplexity_score'] = relationship_data[channel_columns].sum(axis=1)
    else:
        # Alternative: count distinct channels used
        relationship_data['multiplexity_score'] = relationship_data.get('channel_count', 0)

    # Calculate tie strength if individual components available
    tie_strength_components = ['closeness', 'frequency', 'intimacy', 'trust']
    available_components = [col for col in tie_strength_components if col in relationship_data.columns]

    if available_components:
        relationship_data['tie_strength'] = relationship_data[available_components].mean(axis=1)

    analysis_results = {
        'sample_size': len(relationship_data),
        'descriptive_stats': {
            'mean_multiplexity': relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].mean(),
            'std_multiplexity': relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].std(),
            'mean_tie_strength': relationship_data.get('tie_strength', pd.Series([np.nan])).mean(),
            'multiplexity_range': (relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].min(), 
                                 relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].max())
        }
    }

    # Core MMT analysis: Correlation between tie strength and multiplexity
    if 'tie_strength' in relationship_data.columns:
        correlation, p_value = stats.pearsonr(
            relationship_data['tie_strength'].dropna(),
            relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].dropna()
        )

        analysis_results['core_relationship'] = {
            'tie_strength_multiplexity_correlation': correlation,
            'p_value': p_value,
            'effect_size': 'small' if abs(correlation) < 0.3 else 'medium' if abs(correlation) < 0.5 else 'large'
        }

        # Regression analysis
        X = relationship_data[['tie_strength']].fillna(relationship_data[['tie_strength']].mean())
        y = relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].fillna(relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].mean())

        model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)
        r_squared = model.score(X, y)

        analysis_results['regression_analysis'] = {
            'r_squared': r_squared,
            'coefficient': model.coef_[0],
            'intercept': model.intercept_,
            'prediction_equation': f'Multiplexity = {model.intercept_:.2f} + {model.coef_[0]:.2f} * Tie_Strength'
        }

    # Relationship type analysis
    if 'relationship_type' in relationship_data.columns:
        type_analysis = {}

        for rel_type in relationship_data['relationship_type'].unique():
            if pd.notna(rel_type):
                type_data = relationship_data[relationship_data['relationship_type'] == rel_type]
                type_analysis[rel_type] = {
                    'count': len(type_data),
                    'mean_multiplexity': type_data['multiplexity_score'].mean(),
                    'mean_tie_strength': type_data.get('tie_strength', pd.Series([np.nan])).mean(),
                    'std_multiplexity': type_data['multiplexity_score'].std()
                }

        analysis_results['relationship_type_analysis'] = type_analysis

    # Channel-specific analysis
    if channel_columns:
        channel_usage = {}
        for channel in channel_columns:
            usage_rate = relationship_data[channel].mean()
            channel_usage[channel.replace('channel_', '')] = {
                'usage_rate': usage_rate,
                'high_tie_usage': relationship_data[relationship_data.get('tie_strength', 0) > 
                                                  relationship_data.get('tie_strength', pd.Series([0])).median()][channel].mean() if 'tie_strength' in relationship_data.columns else np.nan,
                'low_tie_usage': relationship_data[relationship_data.get('tie_strength', 0) <= 
                                                 relationship_data.get('tie_strength', pd.Series([0])).median()][channel].mean() if 'tie_strength' in relationship_data.columns else np.nan
            }

        analysis_results['channel_analysis'] = channel_usage

    # Multiplexity distribution analysis
    multiplexity_distribution = {}
    for score in range(int(relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].max()) + 1):
        count = (relationship_data['multiplexity_score'] == score).sum()
        percentage = count / len(relationship_data) * 100
        multiplexity_distribution[f'{score}_channels'] = {
            'count': count,
            'percentage': percentage
        }

    analysis_results['multiplexity_distribution'] = multiplexity_distribution

    # Demographic analysis if available
    demographic_vars = ['age', 'gender', 'education', 'technology_comfort']
    available_demographics = [var for var in demographic_vars if var in relationship_data.columns]

    if available_demographics:
        demographic_effects = {}
        for demo_var in available_demographics:
            if relationship_data[demo_var].dtype in ['object', 'category']:
                # Categorical variable
                demo_groups = {}
                for group in relationship_data[demo_var].unique():
                    if pd.notna(group):
                        group_data = relationship_data[relationship_data[demo_var] == group]
                        demo_groups[str(group)] = {
                            'mean_multiplexity': group_data['multiplexity_score'].mean(),
                            'count': len(group_data)
                        }
                demographic_effects[demo_var] = demo_groups
            else:
                # Continuous variable
                correlation, p_val = stats.pearsonr(
                    relationship_data[demo_var].dropna(),
                    relationship_data['multiplexity_score'].dropna()
                )
                demographic_effects[demo_var] = {
                    'correlation_with_multiplexity': correlation,
                    'p_value': p_val
                }

        analysis_results['demographic_effects'] = demographic_effects

    # Generate recommendations
    recommendations = []

    if 'core_relationship' in analysis_results:
        corr = analysis_results['core_relationship']['tie_strength_multiplexity_correlation']
        if corr > 0.4:
            recommendations.append("Strong positive correlation confirms MMT - multiplexity can be used as relationship strength indicator")
        elif corr > 0.2:
            recommendations.append("Moderate correlation supports MMT - consider additional factors affecting multiplexity")
        else:
            recommendations.append("Weak correlation questions MMT applicability - examine context-specific factors")

    if 'relationship_type_analysis' in analysis_results:
        type_data = analysis_results['relationship_type_analysis']
        family_mult = type_data.get('family', {}).get('mean_multiplexity', 0)
        colleague_mult = type_data.get('colleague', {}).get('mean_multiplexity', 0)

        if family_mult > colleague_mult * 1.5:
            recommendations.append("Clear relationship type differences - personal relationships show higher multiplexity")

    if 'channel_analysis' in analysis_results:
        channel_data = analysis_results['channel_analysis']
        low_usage_channels = [ch for ch, data in channel_data.items() if data['usage_rate'] < 0.3]
        if low_usage_channels:
            recommendations.append(f"Underutilized channels identified: {', '.join(low_usage_channels)} - consider promotion or removal")

    analysis_results['recommendations'] = recommendations
    return analysis_results

def simulate_multiplexity_intervention(baseline_data, intervention_type='channel_addition'):
    """
    Simulate effects of introducing new communication channels or relationship interventions

    Parameters:
    baseline_data (DataFrame): Current relationship and multiplexity data
    intervention_type (str): Type of intervention ('channel_addition', 'relationship_building', 'training')

    Returns:
    dict: Predicted intervention outcomes
    """

    np.random.seed(42)
    intervention_data = baseline_data.copy()

    if intervention_type == 'channel_addition':
        # Simulate adding new communication channel
        # Strong ties more likely to adopt new channel
        adoption_probability = 0.3 + 0.5 * (intervention_data.get('tie_strength', 3.5) - 1) / 6
        adoption_probability = np.clip(adoption_probability, 0.1, 0.9)

        new_channel_adoption = np.random.binomial(1, adoption_probability, len(intervention_data))
        intervention_data['new_channel'] = new_channel_adoption
        intervention_data['multiplexity_score'] += new_channel_adoption

        intervention_effect = {
            'intervention_type': 'channel_addition',
            'adoption_rate': new_channel_adoption.mean(),
            'strong_tie_adoption': intervention_data[intervention_data.get('tie_strength', 0) > 4]['new_channel'].mean() if 'tie_strength' in intervention_data.columns else np.nan,
            'weak_tie_adoption': intervention_data[intervention_data.get('tie_strength', 0) <= 4]['new_channel'].mean() if 'tie_strength' in intervention_data.columns else np.nan,
            'multiplexity_increase': intervention_data['multiplexity_score'].mean() - baseline_data['multiplexity_score'].mean()
        }

    elif intervention_type == 'relationship_building':
        # Simulate relationship strengthening leading to multiplexity increase
        relationship_improvement = np.random.normal(0.3, 0.15, len(intervention_data))
        relationship_improvement = np.clip(relationship_improvement, 0, 1)

        if 'tie_strength' in intervention_data.columns:
            intervention_data['tie_strength'] += relationship_improvement
            intervention_data['tie_strength'] = np.clip(intervention_data['tie_strength'], 1, 7)

            # Multiplexity increases with relationship strength (MMT prediction)
            multiplexity_increase = relationship_improvement * 0.8  # Scaling factor
            intervention_data['multiplexity_score'] += multiplexity_increase

        intervention_effect = {
            'intervention_type': 'relationship_building',
            'mean_relationship_improvement': relationship_improvement.mean(),
            'mean_multiplexity_increase': (intervention_data['multiplexity_score'].mean() - 
                                         baseline_data['multiplexity_score'].mean()),
            'correlation_change': 'Strengthened relationships should show higher multiplexity'
        }

    elif intervention_type == 'training':
        # Simulate communication technology training
        training_effect = np.random.normal(0.4, 0.2, len(intervention_data))
        training_effect = np.clip(training_effect, 0, 2)

        # Training increases multiplexity more for those with lower baseline
        baseline_multiplexity = intervention_data['multiplexity_score']
        training_boost = training_effect * (1 - baseline_multiplexity / baseline_multiplexity.max())

        intervention_data['multiplexity_score'] += training_boost

        intervention_effect = {
            'intervention_type': 'training',
            'mean_training_effect': training_effect.mean(),
            'multiplexity_improvement': training_boost.mean(),
            'baseline_effect_correlation': stats.pearsonr(baseline_multiplexity, training_boost)[0]
        }

    return intervention_effect, intervention_data

def create_multiplexity_network_visualization(relationship_data):
    """
    Create network visualization showing relationships and multiplexity patterns

    Parameters:
    relationship_data (DataFrame): Relationship data with source, target, tie_strength, multiplexity

    Returns:
    NetworkX graph object and visualization
    """

    # Create network graph
    G = nx.Graph()

    # Add edges with attributes
    for _, row in relationship_data.iterrows():
        if 'source' in row and 'target' in row:
            G.add_edge(
                row['source'], 
                row['target'],
                tie_strength=row.get('tie_strength', 1),
                multiplexity=row.get('multiplexity_score', 1)
            )

    # Set node and edge attributes for visualization
    node_sizes = [G.degree(node) * 100 for node in G.nodes()]
    edge_weights = [G[u][v].get('multiplexity', 1) for u, v in G.edges()]
    edge_colors = [G[u][v].get('tie_strength', 1) for u, v in G.edges()]

    # Create visualization
    plt.figure(figsize=(12, 8))
    pos = nx.spring_layout(G, k=1, iterations=50)

    # Draw network
    nx.draw_networkx_nodes(G, pos, node_size=node_sizes, node_color='lightblue', alpha=0.7)
    nx.draw_networkx_edges(G, pos, width=edge_weights, edge_color=edge_colors, 
                          edge_cmap=plt.cm.Blues, alpha=0.6)
    nx.draw_networkx_labels(G, pos, font_size=8)

    plt.title('Social Network with Media Multiplexity\n(Edge width = Multiplexity, Edge color = Tie Strength)')
    plt.axis('off')

    return G
