    Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT)

    1) Executive Summary¶

    Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT), developed by W. Timothy Coombs in 2007, provides a prescriptive framework for selecting appropriate crisis communication strategies based on crisis type and attributed responsibility. Unlike descriptive approaches, SCCT offers evidence-based recommendations by matching three crisis clusters (victim, accident, preventable) with three response strategy postures (denial, diminish, rebuild). The theory integrates attribution theory principles, recognizing that stakeholder perceptions of organizational responsibility determine appropriate response strategies. Crisis responsibility serves as the central construct, with victim crises generating minimal responsibility attribution, accident crises creating moderate responsibility, and preventable crises producing high responsibility attribution. SCCT incorporates intensifying factors including crisis history, prior reputation, and crisis severity that can increase responsibility attributions and necessitate stronger response strategies. The theory emphasizes reputation protection as the primary goal, recommending accommodative strategies (accept responsibility, provide aid) for high-responsibility crises and defensive strategies (denial, excuse) for low-responsibility situations. SCCT has been extensively validated across multiple crisis contexts and provides practical guidance for crisis managers seeking to minimize reputational damage through strategic communication choices.

    2) Definition & Scope¶

    • Definition: A prescriptive theory that provides evidence-based recommendations for selecting crisis communication strategies by matching response approaches to crisis types based on stakeholder attributions of organizational responsibility and crisis characteristics.
    • Scope: Organizational crisis communication contexts including corporate crises, product recalls, safety incidents, misconduct scandals, natural disasters affecting organizations, and any situation requiring strategic communication response to protect organizational reputation.

    3) Key Constructs & Propositions¶

    Central Theoretical Foundation¶

    Attribution Theory Integration¶

    • Definition: SCCT applies attribution theory principles to understand how stakeholders assign responsibility for crisis events
    • Causal Attribution Process: Stakeholders evaluate crisis events to determine organizational responsibility levels
    • Responsibility Continuum: Ranges from no responsibility (victim) to high responsibility (preventable)
    • Strategic Implication: Response strategies must address stakeholder responsibility attributions to be effective

    Crisis Responsibility¶

    • Definition: The degree to which stakeholders hold the organization accountable for the crisis event and its consequences
    • Attribution Factors: Intent, foreseeability, controllability, and organizational capability
    • Measurement Dimensions: Causal responsibility, treatment responsibility, blame assignment
    • Strategic Significance: Primary determinant of appropriate response strategy selection

    Crisis Type Classification System¶

    Victim Crisis Cluster (Minimal Responsibility)¶

    Characteristics: Organization portrayed as victim of crisis event with minimal control over situation

    Natural Disasters - Definition: Weather-related or geological events affecting organizational operations - Attribution Pattern: Low organizational responsibility, external causation - Stakeholder Sympathy: High levels of public sympathy and support - Example Applications: Hurricanes, earthquakes, floods disrupting business operations - Strategic Implications: Organization can maintain victim status if response is appropriate

    Rumors - Definition: False information circulating about organization or its products/services - Attribution Pattern: Organization as target rather than cause of problem - Response Requirements: Correct misinformation while avoiding amplification - Example Applications: False product contamination claims, unfounded safety rumors - Strategic Considerations: Quick response needed to prevent rumor solidification

    Workplace Violence - Definition: Violence committed by external parties against organizational members - Attribution Pattern: Organization and employees viewed as victims - Responsibility Limitations: Limited organizational control over external actors - Example Applications: Customer attacks on employees, external threats to facilities - Response Focus: Employee safety, support for victims, security measures

    Product Tampering/Malevolence - Definition: External parties intentionally damaging products or services - Attribution Pattern: Criminal activity targeting organization - Victim Status: Clear distinction between organization and perpetrator - Example Applications: Product contamination by external actors, cyberattacks - Strategic Approach: Cooperation with authorities, victim support, security enhancement

    Accident Crisis Cluster (Moderate Responsibility)¶

    Characteristics: Organization has some responsibility but crisis viewed as unintentional

    Challenges - Definition: Stakeholder claims that organization operates inappropriately - Attribution Complexity: Disputed responsibility between organization and accusers - Evidence Requirements: Facts needed to determine actual organizational responsibility - Example Applications: Environmental group challenges, consumer complaints - Strategic Considerations: Fact-based response, avoid escalation

    Technical-Error Accidents - Definition: Technology or equipment failure causes crisis without organizational intent - Attribution Pattern: Moderate responsibility for maintenance and oversight - Foreseeability Factors: Whether organization should have anticipated failure - Example Applications: Software glitches, equipment malfunctions, system failures - Response Elements: Technical explanation, corrective action, prevention measures

    Technical-Error Recalls - Definition: Product recalls due to discovered defects or safety issues - Attribution Considerations: Organizational responsibility for quality control - Stakeholder Expectations: Prompt action to protect public safety - Example Applications: Auto recalls, pharmaceutical withdrawals, device malfunctions - Strategic Requirements: Consumer safety priority, comprehensive corrective action

    Preventable Crisis Cluster (High Responsibility)¶

    Characteristics: Organization has high responsibility, crisis viewed as intentional or preventable

    Human-Error Accidents - Definition: Organizational member errors leading to crisis situations - Attribution Pattern: High responsibility for employee training and oversight - Preventability Perception: Stakeholders believe proper procedures could have prevented crisis - Example Applications: Industrial accidents, transportation crashes, operational errors - Response Necessity: Accept responsibility, comprehensive corrective action

    Human-Error Recalls - Definition: Product recalls due to organizational member mistakes - Attribution Elements: Responsibility for quality control and supervision - Stakeholder Expectations: Acknowledgment of error, systemic improvements - Example Applications: Contaminated food products, mislabeled medications - Strategic Approach: Responsibility acceptance, consumer protection priority

    Organizational Misdeed - Definition: Intentional actions by organization that stakeholders view as inappropriate - Attribution Certainty: Clear organizational responsibility and intentionality - Moral Dimensions: Ethical and legal violations affecting stakeholder trust - Example Applications: Financial fraud, environmental violations, discrimination - Response Requirements: Full accountability, comprehensive reform measures

    Crisis Response Strategy Framework¶

    Denial Posture Strategies (Low Responsibility)¶

    Strategic Approach: Minimize or eliminate organizational responsibility attribution

    Attack the Accuser - Definition: Challenge credibility or motivations of those making accusations - Usage Conditions: When accusations are demonstrably false or motivations questionable - Implementation Requirements: Strong evidence supporting organization's position - Risk Factors: Can appear defensive and may escalate conflict - Effectiveness Context: Most effective with victim crisis cluster

    Denial - Definition: Assert that crisis did not occur or organization not involved - Application Scope: Situations with insufficient evidence or false accusations - Credibility Requirements: Must be sustainable if challenged with evidence - Strategic Timing: Most effective when implemented quickly - Legal Considerations: Coordination with legal counsel essential

    Scapegoat - Definition: Blame external party or individual for crisis occurrence - Implementation Strategy: Identify credible alternative explanation for crisis - Evidence Necessity: Must provide convincing case for alternative responsibility - Relationship Impact: May damage relationships with scapegoated parties - Example Applications: Contractor negligence, supplier failures, individual misconduct

    Diminish Posture Strategies (Moderate Responsibility)¶

    Strategic Approach: Accept some responsibility while reducing perceived severity

    Excuse - Definition: Minimize organizational responsibility by citing external factors - Justification Elements: Lack of control, information, or intent - Implementation Requirements: Credible evidence supporting limited responsibility - Stakeholder Acceptance: Must resonate with stakeholder understanding of situation - Strategic Effectiveness: Works well with accident crisis cluster

    Justification - Definition: Accept responsibility while minimizing perceived damage - Damage Minimization: Reduce perception of harm severity or scope - Perspective Shifting: Frame consequences in less negative light - Evidence Requirements: Factual basis for minimization claims - Risk Considerations: Can appear callous if minimization seems inappropriate

    Rebuild Posture Strategies (High Responsibility)¶

    Strategic Approach: Accept responsibility and work to repair relationships

    Compensation - Definition: Provide money, goods, or services to crisis victims - Value Demonstration: Tangible evidence of concern and responsibility acceptance - Implementation Scope: Individual victim compensation or community investment - Legal Coordination: Must consider litigation implications - Stakeholder Signal: Demonstrates commitment to making things right

    Apology - Definition: Accept full responsibility and ask forgiveness from stakeholders - Authenticity Requirements: Must appear genuine rather than strategic - Completeness Factors: Comprehensive acknowledgment of harm and responsibility - Cultural Considerations: Effectiveness varies across cultural contexts - Legal Implications: May create legal liability in some jurisdictions

    Bolstering Strategies (Supplemental)¶

    Strategic Approach: Remind stakeholders of organization's positive attributes

    Reminder - Definition: Tell stakeholders about past good works and positive contributions - Historical Emphasis: Highlight track record of responsible behavior - Context Creation: Provide broader perspective on organizational character - Implementation Timing: Can be combined with other strategies - Effectiveness Factors: Past performance must be substantial and relevant

    Ingratiation - Definition: Praise stakeholders and express concern for their welfare - Relationship Building: Demonstrate care and connection with affected parties - Stakeholder Values: Acknowledge and align with stakeholder priorities - Authenticity Requirements: Must appear genuine rather than manipulative - Application Context: Effective when combined with substantive actions

    Victimage - Definition: Remind stakeholders that organization is also crisis victim - Shared Suffering: Emphasize organizational losses and impacts - Sympathy Generation: Seek stakeholder understanding and support - Implementation Cautions: Must not appear to minimize stakeholder harm - Strategic Timing: Most effective early in crisis response

    Intensifying Factors¶

    Crisis History¶

    • Definition: Organization's track record of similar crises in recent past
    • Attribution Impact: Previous crises increase responsibility attribution for current crisis
    • Stakeholder Memory: Recent crises more influential than distant history
    • Strategic Implication: Organizations with crisis history need stronger response strategies
    • Measurement Period: Typically assessed over 2-3 year timeframe

    Prior Reputation¶

    • Definition: Stakeholder perceptions of organization before current crisis
    • Protective Effect: Positive reputation provides some protection against responsibility attribution
    • Vulnerability Factor: Negative reputation intensifies responsibility attribution
    • Relationship Quality: Strong stakeholder relationships buffer crisis impact
    • Assessment Dimensions: Competence, trustworthiness, goodwill evaluations

    Crisis Severity¶

    • Definition: Amount of damage caused by crisis event
    • Attribution Amplification: More severe crises generate stronger responsibility attributions
    • Stakeholder Impact: Physical, financial, and emotional harm to stakeholders
    • Media Attention: Severity correlates with media coverage intensity
    • Response Escalation: Severe crises require more accommodative response strategies

    Core Propositions¶

    P1: Crisis type determines baseline level of crisis responsibility attributed to organization

    P2: Appropriate response strategies match the level of crisis responsibility attribution

    P3: Victim crises require denial posture strategies to maintain low responsibility attribution

    P4: Accident crises require diminish posture strategies to manage moderate responsibility attribution

    P5: Preventable crises require rebuild posture strategies to address high responsibility attribution

    P6: Intensifying factors (history, reputation, severity) can increase responsibility attribution and necessitate stronger response strategies

    P7: Mismatched strategies (weak response to high responsibility crisis) result in greater reputational damage

    P8: Bolstering strategies can be combined with other strategies to enhance overall response effectiveness

    5) Boundary Conditions & Assumptions¶

    Core Assumptions¶

    • Rational Stakeholders: Stakeholders make logical attributions about crisis responsibility based on available information
    • Reputation Value: Organizational reputation is a valuable asset requiring strategic protection
    • Strategy Effectiveness: Appropriate crisis response strategies can minimize reputational damage
    • Attribution Consistency: Similar crisis types generate similar responsibility attributions across stakeholder groups
    • Communication Impact: Strategic communication significantly influences stakeholder perceptions

    Boundary Conditions¶

    • Cultural Context: Theory developed in Western contexts; effectiveness may vary across cultures with different communication norms
    • Crisis Complexity: Some crises may not fit neatly into the three-cluster system
    • Stakeholder Diversity: Different stakeholder groups may attribute responsibility differently for the same crisis
    • Legal Constraints: Legal considerations may limit strategy options, particularly regarding apologies and admissions
    • Resource Limitations: Some strategies require significant organizational resources that may not be available
    • Media Environment: Digital and social media may accelerate attribution processes and limit response time
    • Organizational Structure: Multinational organizations may face competing stakeholder expectations across different markets

    7) Research Designs & Analysis¶

    Experimental Design Framework¶

    Basic SCCT Experiment: - Independent Variables: Crisis Type (3 levels: victim, accident, preventable) × Response Strategy (3 levels: denial, diminish, rebuild) - Control Variables: Organization type, industry context, participant demographics - Dependent Variables: Crisis responsibility attribution, organizational reputation, behavioral intentions - Design: 3×3 between-subjects factorial design with random assignment

    Advanced Experimental Design¶

    Full SCCT Model Test: 

    4×4×3×2 Factorial Design:
- Crisis Type (4 levels: natural disaster, technical error, human error, misdeed)
- Response Strategy (4 levels: denial, excuse, apology, compensation)
- Prior Reputation (3 levels: positive, neutral, negative)  
- Crisis History (2 levels: no history, previous crisis)

    Dependent Variable Battery: - Crisis responsibility attribution (5-item scale) - Organizational reputation (15-item scale) - Stakeholder trust (7-item scale) - Purchase/support intentions (4-item scale) - Emotional responses (anger, sympathy, fear)

    Longitudinal Field Study Protocol¶

    Phase 1: Pre-Crisis Baseline (Ongoing) - Monitor organizational reputation across stakeholder groups - Track media sentiment and stakeholder relationship quality - Document organizational characteristics and vulnerability factors

    Phase 2: Crisis Response Analysis (Real-time) - Code crisis type using SCCT classification system - Analyze response strategies using content analysis - Monitor stakeholder reactions across multiple channels - Track media coverage and public opinion

    Phase 3: Post-Crisis Recovery Assessment (6-12 months) - Measure reputation recovery using baseline comparisons - Assess long-term stakeholder relationship changes - Evaluate strategy effectiveness through outcome analysis - Document lessons learned and recommendations

    Crisis Type and Attribution Patterns¶

    Victim Crisis Cluster: - Natural Disasters: Crisis responsibility M = 2.1 (SD = 1.3) on 7-point scale - Rumors: Crisis responsibility M = 1.8 (SD = 1.1), highest stakeholder sympathy - Product Tampering: Crisis responsibility M = 2.3 (SD = 1.4), clear external causation - Workplace Violence: Crisis responsibility M = 2.0 (SD = 1.2), organization as victim

    Accident Crisis Cluster: - Challenges: Crisis responsibility M = 3.8 (SD = 1.6), disputed attribution - Technical Errors: Crisis responsibility M = 4.2 (SD = 1.5), moderate organizational responsibility - Technical Recalls: Crisis responsibility M = 4.0 (SD = 1.4), quality control expectations

    Preventable Crisis Cluster: - Human Error: Crisis responsibility M = 5.6 (SD = 1.3), high training/oversight responsibility - Organizational Misdeed: Crisis responsibility M = 6.2 (SD = 1.1), highest attribution levels - Human Error Recalls: Crisis responsibility M = 5.4 (SD = 1.4), preventability perceptions

    Strategy Effectiveness by Crisis Type¶

    Victim Crises + Denial Strategies: - Reputation maintenance: 85% of pre-crisis levels - Stakeholder support intentions: 78% positive response - Strategy appropriateness ratings: M = 5.8/7.0

    Accident Crises + Diminish Strategies: - Reputation recovery: 72% of pre-crisis levels - Stakeholder trust: 65% maintenance rate - Strategy appropriateness: M = 5.2/7.0

    Preventable Crises + Rebuild Strategies: - Reputation recovery: 58% of pre-crisis levels (6 months post-crisis) - Long-term stakeholder loyalty: 61% retention rate - Strategy appropriateness: M = 6.1/7.0

    Intensifying Factor Effects¶

    Crisis History Impact: - No History: Baseline responsibility attribution levels - One Previous Crisis: 12-15% increase in responsibility attribution - Multiple Crises: 25-30% increase in responsibility attribution - Strategy Escalation: 67% of organizations with history require stronger strategies

    Prior Reputation Effects: - Positive Reputation: 18-22% reduction in responsibility attribution - Neutral Reputation: Baseline attribution patterns - Negative Reputation: 20-25% increase in responsibility attribution - Recovery Speed: Positive reputation organizations recover 40% faster

    Crisis Severity Correlations: - High severity crises show 35% stronger responsibility attributions - Media coverage intensity correlates r = .67 with severity perceptions - Stakeholder emotional responses amplify with severity (anger: r = .72)

    Cultural and Contextual Variations¶

    Individualistic Cultures (US, Western Europe): - Stronger individual accountability expectations - Higher effectiveness of apology strategies - Clear responsibility attribution patterns

    Collectivistic Cultures (East Asia, Latin America): - Group harmony considerations affect attribution - Face-saving important in strategy selection - Relationship restoration prioritized over blame assignment

    Legal System Influences: - Common law systems: 45% higher denial strategy usage - Civil law systems: 35% higher apology strategy usage - Regulatory environments affect corrective action requirements

    9) Applications¶

    Crisis Communication Strategy Selection Framework¶

    Step 1: Crisis Type Classification (Within 2 hours) 

    Assessment Questions:
1. Was the crisis caused by external forces beyond organizational control? → Victim
2. Was the crisis unintentional but organizationally related? → Accident  
3. Could the organization have prevented the crisis through different actions? → Preventable
4. Is there clear evidence of organizational intent or negligence? → Preventable

    Step 2: Intensifying Factor Evaluation (Within 4 hours) - Crisis History Review: Document similar crises in past 2-3 years - Reputation Assessment: Current stakeholder perception levels - Severity Analysis: Scope and scale of crisis impacts - Attribution Adjustment: Increase strategy strength if intensifying factors present

    Step 3: Strategy Selection Matrix 

    Base Strategy Selection:
Victim Crisis + No Intensifying Factors = Denial Posture
Victim Crisis + Intensifying Factors = Diminish Posture
Accident Crisis + No Intensifying Factors = Diminish Posture  
Accident Crisis + Intensifying Factors = Rebuild Posture
Preventable Crisis + Any Factors = Rebuild Posture

    Step 4: Strategy Implementation Protocol - Primary Strategy: Select appropriate posture strategy as foundation - Bolstering Addition: Add reminder, ingratiation, or victimage as appropriate - Message Consistency: Ensure all communications align with selected strategy - Monitoring System: Track stakeholder response and adjust if necessary

    Industry-Specific Application Guidelines¶

    Technology Sector: - Data Breaches: Typically accident crises requiring diminish/rebuild strategies - Software Failures: Technical errors needing excuse + corrective action - Privacy Issues: May be preventable requiring apology + system changes

    Healthcare Industry: - Patient Safety: Usually preventable requiring full apology + compensation - Data Privacy: HIPAA violations need rebuild strategies with legal coordination - Quality Issues: Technical errors requiring excuse + prevention measures

    Financial Services: - Market Losses: Often accident crises using excuse strategies - Misconduct: Clearly preventable requiring apology + comprehensive reform - System Failures: Technical errors needing excuse + reliability improvements

    Manufacturing: - Product Defects: Technical recalls using excuse + quality improvements - Safety Incidents: Human error requiring apology + safety protocol changes - Environmental Issues: Often preventable needing rebuild + environmental programs

    Crisis Training and Preparation¶

    SCCT-Based Crisis Simulation: - Scenario Development: Create industry-relevant crisis scenarios across all three clusters - Strategy Practice: Train teams on appropriate strategy selection and implementation - Decision Trees: Develop organizational-specific decision frameworks - Stakeholder Mapping: Identify key audiences and likely attribution patterns

    Pre-Crisis Planning: - Vulnerability Assessment: Identify potential crises by type and likelihood - Strategy Templates: Pre-develop message frameworks for each crisis cluster - Approval Processes: Establish decision-making authority for different strategy levels - Resource Allocation: Ensure capabilities for rebuild strategies if needed

    11) Critiques & Debates¶

    Major Criticisms¶

    Oversimplified Crisis Classification: Critics argue that many crises don't fit neatly into the three-cluster system, with hybrid characteristics requiring more nuanced analysis

    Limited Cultural Validation: Theory developed primarily in Western contexts with limited testing across diverse cultural communication patterns and attribution processes

    Static Response Approach: SCCT doesn't adequately address how strategies should evolve throughout crisis lifecycle or adapt to changing stakeholder reactions

    Stakeholder Homogenization: Theory treats stakeholder groups as relatively uniform in attribution patterns, not accounting for significant differences in responsibility perceptions

    Contemporary Debates¶

    Digital Communication Challenges: - Real-time Response Pressure: Social media demands immediate responses that may not allow careful SCCT analysis - Multiple Audience Complexity: Different stakeholder groups may receive different messages simultaneously online - Viral Information Spread: Traditional crisis communication control assumptions challenged by viral sharing

    Stakeholder Empowerment Impact: - Increased Expectations: Modern stakeholders expect higher accountability standards - Activist Amplification: Organized stakeholder groups can reject traditional crisis response approaches - Economic Pressure: Boycott threats make strategy selection more complex

    Legal and Ethical Evolution: - Admission Liability: Legal implications of apology strategies vary across jurisdictions - Transparency Demands: Stakeholder expectations for information disclosure conflict with legal caution - Corporate Social Responsibility: ESG expectations affect crisis response appropriateness

    Theoretical Developments¶

    Multi-Stage Crisis Response: Research developing dynamic SCCT models that account for strategy evolution throughout crisis lifecycle

    Stakeholder-Specific SCCT: Adaptations recognizing different stakeholder groups may require different strategy approaches simultaneously

    Digital Age SCCT: Modifications addressing social media, real-time communication, and changed stakeholder expectations

    Cultural SCCT Variants: Development of culturally-adapted versions accounting for different attribution patterns and communication norms

    12) Cross-Cultural, Ethics, DEI¶

    Cultural Adaptations¶

    High-Context Cultures: - Indirect Responsibility Acknowledgment: Face-saving considerations require subtle admission approaches - Relationship Priority: Harmony restoration more important than blame assignment - Authority Expectations: Senior leadership involvement essential in crisis response

    Low-Context Cultures: - Direct Responsibility Statements: Clear, explicit acknowledgment expected - Fact-Based Responses: Evidence and logical reasoning prioritized - Individual Accountability: Personal responsibility acceptance valued

    Collectivistic Orientations: - Group Responsibility: Organizational rather than individual accountability emphasized - Community Impact: Broader social consequences considered in strategy selection - Consensus Building: Stakeholder consultation expected in response development

    Power Distance Considerations: - High Power Distance: Authority figures expected to take clear responsibility and provide direction - Low Power Distance: Collaborative approach to problem-solving and shared responsibility expected

    Ethical Framework¶

    Stakeholder Welfare Priority: - Harm Mitigation: Response strategies should prioritize reducing stakeholder harm over organizational protection - Truth and Transparency: Honest communication about crisis causes and organizational responsibility - Vulnerable Population Protection: Special consideration for stakeholders unable to protect themselves

    Organizational Responsibility: - Accountability Acceptance: Appropriate responsibility acknowledgment based on actual organizational role - Corrective Action Commitment: Genuine efforts to address problems and prevent recurrence - Resource Allocation: Fair distribution of crisis response resources to affected stakeholders

    Professional Standards: - Competent Counsel: Communication professionals should recommend appropriate strategies based on evidence - Conflict Management: Clear protocols for situations involving competing stakeholder interests - Long-term Perspective: Strategies should support sustainable stakeholder relationships

    Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Integration¶

    Inclusive Crisis Assessment: - Diverse Perspective Integration: Include diverse voices in crisis type classification and strategy selection - Cultural Competence: Consider how different cultural groups may attribute responsibility differently - Accessibility: Ensure crisis communications are accessible to all stakeholder groups

    Equitable Response Implementation: - Differential Impact Analysis: Assess how crisis affects different demographic groups differently - Resource Distribution: Ensure crisis response resources reach all affected communities - Participation Opportunities: Create mechanisms for diverse stakeholder input in response planning

    Community-Centered Approach: - Local Knowledge Integration: Incorporate community expertise in strategy development - Culturally Appropriate Messaging: Adapt crisis communications to diverse cultural contexts - Long-term Relationship Building: Focus on sustainable relationships with diverse stakeholder communities

    SCCT-Based Crisis Response Protocol¶

    Phase 1: Immediate Assessment (0-2 hours) 

    # SCCT Crisis Classification Checklist
def classify_crisis_type(crisis_characteristics):
    """Systematic crisis type classification"""

    # Victim Crisis Indicators
    victim_indicators = [
        "External actor caused crisis",
        "Organization had no control",
        "Organization is target/victim",
        "Natural disaster or external force"
    ]

    # Accident Crisis Indicators  
    accident_indicators = [
        "Unintentional organizational action",
        "Technical failure or error", 
        "Some organizational involvement",
        "Disputed responsibility"
    ]

    # Preventable Crisis Indicators
    preventable_indicators = [
        "Organizational member error",
        "Intentional inappropriate action",
        "Preventable through proper procedures",
        "Clear organizational responsibility"
    ]

    # Score each category (implement actual logic)
    victim_score = sum([1 for indicator in victim_indicators if indicator in crisis_characteristics])
    accident_score = sum([1 for indicator in accident_indicators if indicator in crisis_characteristics])
    preventable_score = sum([1 for indicator in preventable_indicators if indicator in crisis_characteristics])

    # Return primary classification
    if victim_score >= accident_score and victim_score >= preventable_score:
        return "victim"
    elif accident_score >= preventable_score:
        return "accident"  
    else:
        return "preventable"

    Phase 2: Intensifying Factor Analysis (2-4 hours) - Crisis History Review: Document similar crises in past 2-3 years, assess pattern impact - Reputation Assessment: Survey key stakeholders or use existing reputation data - Severity Evaluation: Quantify physical, financial, and emotional harm to stakeholders - Strategy Adjustment: Escalate strategy strength based on intensifying factor presence

    Phase 3: Strategy Selection and Implementation (4-8 hours) 

    SCCT Strategy Selection Matrix:

Victim Crisis:
- No Intensifying Factors: Denial Posture (Attack/Denial/Scapegoat)
- Intensifying Factors Present: Diminish Posture (Excuse/Justification)

Accident Crisis:  
- No Intensifying Factors: Diminish Posture (Excuse/Justification)
- Intensifying Factors Present: Rebuild Posture (Compensation/Apology)

Preventable Crisis:
- Any Factor Configuration: Rebuild Posture (Compensation/Apology)

Bolstering Additions:
- Add Reminder for organizations with strong positive history
- Add Ingratiation when stakeholder relationships are key
- Add Victimage when organization also suffered losses

    Phase 4: Message Development and Deployment - Primary Strategy Messages: Core communications emphasizing selected strategy approach - Stakeholder Customization: Adapt messages for different audiences while maintaining consistency - Channel Optimization: Tailor message format for traditional media, social media, direct communication - Spokesperson Preparation: Brief all potential speakers on key messages and strategy rationale

    Digital Age SCCT Implementation¶

    Social Media Adaptation Protocol: - Speed Requirements: Pre-develop response templates for common crisis scenarios by type - Platform Customization: Adapt SCCT strategies for Twitter (concise), Facebook (detailed), LinkedIn (professional) - Real-time Monitoring: Track stakeholder attribution patterns across platforms to validate strategy selection - Viral Risk Management: Procedures for rapid strategy adjustment if messages are misinterpreted

    Multi-Channel Consistency Framework: - Message Coordination: Ensure identical core strategy across all communication channels - Timing Synchronization: Coordinate release of primary messages across platforms - Feedback Integration: Monitor stakeholder responses to validate strategy appropriateness - Adaptation Protocols: Guidelines for strategy modification based on real-time feedback

    Long-term Reputation Recovery Using SCCT¶

    Months 1-3: Strategy Reinforcement - Continue implementation of selected SCCT strategy with consistency - Monitor reputation recovery through regular stakeholder surveys - Adjust bolstering strategies based on stakeholder feedback - Document lessons learned for future crisis preparedness

    Months 4-6: Relationship Rebuilding - Implement sustained corrective actions if rebuild strategy was used - Engage in proactive stakeholder relationship building activities - Demonstrate organizational learning through policy and procedure changes - Measure behavior change through stakeholder engagement metrics

    Months 7-12: Prevention and Preparation - Use crisis experience to improve organizational crisis preparedness - Develop SCCT-based crisis response plans for potential future crises - Train organizational members on SCCT principles and application - Establish ongoing reputation monitoring and early warning systems

    import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def scct_crisis_analysis(crisis_data, stakeholder_responses):
    """
    Comprehensive SCCT analysis including crisis classification, 
    strategy effectiveness, and reputation impact assessment

    Parameters:
    crisis_data (dict): Crisis characteristics and organizational response
    stakeholder_responses (DataFrame): Stakeholder survey data

    Returns:
    dict: Complete SCCT analysis with recommendations
    """

    # Crisis Type Classification
    def classify_crisis(characteristics):
        victim_keywords = ['external', 'attack', 'natural', 'tampering', 'rumor']
        accident_keywords = ['technical', 'error', 'failure', 'challenge', 'unintentional']
        preventable_keywords = ['misconduct', 'negligence', 'violation', 'intentional']

        victim_score = sum([1 for keyword in victim_keywords if keyword in characteristics.lower()])
        accident_score = sum([1 for keyword in accident_keywords if keyword in characteristics.lower()])
        preventable_score = sum([1 for keyword in preventable_keywords if keyword in characteristics.lower()])

        scores = {'victim': victim_score, 'accident': accident_score, 'preventable': preventable_score}
        return max(scores.items(), key=lambda x: x[1])[0]

    crisis_type = classify_crisis(crisis_data['description'])

    # Analyze Intensifying Factors
    intensifying_factors = {}
    intensifying_factors['crisis_history'] = crisis_data.get('previous_crises', 0) > 0
    intensifying_factors['prior_reputation'] = crisis_data.get('reputation_score', 5.0) < 4.0
    intensifying_factors['crisis_severity'] = crisis_data.get('severity_score', 3.0) > 5.0

    intensification_level = sum(intensifying_factors.values())

    # Strategy Appropriateness Assessment
    actual_strategy = crisis_data.get('response_strategy', 'unknown')

    # SCCT Strategy Recommendations
    if crisis_type == 'victim' and intensification_level == 0:
        recommended_strategy = 'denial'
        expected_effectiveness = 0.85
    elif crisis_type == 'victim' and intensification_level > 0:
        recommended_strategy = 'diminish'  
        expected_effectiveness = 0.75
    elif crisis_type == 'accident' and intensification_level == 0:
        recommended_strategy = 'diminish'
        expected_effectiveness = 0.70
    elif crisis_type == 'accident' and intensification_level > 0:
        recommended_strategy = 'rebuild'
        expected_effectiveness = 0.65
    else:  # preventable
        recommended_strategy = 'rebuild'
        expected_effectiveness = 0.60

    # Strategy Match Analysis
    strategy_match = actual_strategy.lower() == recommended_strategy.lower()

    # Stakeholder Response Analysis
    if not stakeholder_responses.empty:
        responsibility_attribution = stakeholder_responses['crisis_responsibility'].mean()
        reputation_score = stakeholder_responses['reputation'].mean()
        behavioral_intentions = stakeholder_responses['behavioral_intentions'].mean()

        # Calculate strategy effectiveness
        if strategy_match:
            actual_effectiveness = min(expected_effectiveness + 0.1, 1.0)
        else:
            actual_effectiveness = max(expected_effectiveness - 0.2, 0.0)
    else:
        responsibility_attribution = None
        reputation_score = None
        behavioral_intentions = None
        actual_effectiveness = expected_effectiveness

    # Generate Recommendations
    recommendations = []

    if not strategy_match:
        recommendations.append(f"Strategy mismatch: Used {actual_strategy}, should use {recommended_strategy}")

    if intensification_level > 1:
        recommendations.append("Multiple intensifying factors present - consider stronger response strategies")

    if responsibility_attribution and responsibility_attribution > 5.0:
        recommendations.append("High responsibility attribution - rebuild strategies recommended")

    if reputation_score and reputation_score < 4.0:
        recommendations.append("Reputation significantly damaged - implement comprehensive recovery plan")

    return {
        'crisis_classification': {
            'type': crisis_type,
            'intensifying_factors': intensifying_factors,
            'intensification_level': intensification_level
        },
        'strategy_analysis': {
            'actual_strategy': actual_strategy,
            'recommended_strategy': recommended_strategy,
            'strategy_match': strategy_match,
            'expected_effectiveness': expected_effectiveness,
            'actual_effectiveness': actual_effectiveness
        },
        'stakeholder_impact': {
            'responsibility_attribution': responsibility_attribution,
            'reputation_score': reputation_score,
            'behavioral_intentions': behavioral_intentions
        },
        'recommendations': recommendations
    }

def scct_effectiveness_simulation(crisis_types, strategies, n_simulations=1000):
    """
    Monte Carlo simulation of SCCT strategy effectiveness
    across different crisis types and response strategies
    """

    # Base effectiveness rates from research
    effectiveness_matrix = {
        ('victim', 'denial'): 0.85,
        ('victim', 'diminish'): 0.75,  
        ('victim', 'rebuild'): 0.70,
        ('accident', 'denial'): 0.45,
        ('accident', 'diminish'): 0.70,
        ('accident', 'rebuild'): 0.65,
        ('preventable', 'denial'): 0.25,
        ('preventable', 'diminish'): 0.40,
        ('preventable', 'rebuild'): 0.60
    }

    results = []

    for crisis_type in crisis_types:
        for strategy in strategies:
            base_effectiveness = effectiveness_matrix.get((crisis_type, strategy), 0.50)

            # Add random variation (± 15%)
            simulated_results = []
            for _ in range(n_simulations):
                variation = np.random.normal(0, 0.15)
                effectiveness = np.clip(base_effectiveness + variation, 0, 1)
                simulated_results.append(effectiveness)

            results.append({
                'crisis_type': crisis_type,
                'strategy': strategy,
                'mean_effectiveness': np.mean(simulated_results),
                'std_effectiveness': np.std(simulated_results),
                'optimal_match': base_effectiveness == max([effectiveness_matrix.get((crisis_type, s), 0) for s in strategies])
            })

    return pd.DataFrame(results)
